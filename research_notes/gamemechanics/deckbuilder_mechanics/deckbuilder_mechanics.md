# Deck-builders share one loop, then diverge

Across about 70 deck-, bag- and dice-building board games, the mechanics fall into one stable core and roughly fourteen families of variation around it. The core, set by **Dominion (2008)**, is an identical weak starting deck, purchases from a shared market into the discard pile, a reshuffle when the draw pile runs out, and a per-turn budget of actions and buys ([Wikipedia: Dominion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion_(card_game))). Everything since changes *what* the market is (fixed piles, rotating row, pyramid, role stacks, opponents' leftovers), *what* the currency buys (cards versus fights versus board actions), *how* cards leave or improve (trash, scrap, exile, entomb, promote, upgrade, sleeve-craft, flip), *what clogs the deck* (Victory cards, curses, wounds, heat, unrest, fear, fog of war), and *what ends the game* (pile-out, shared pool exhaustion, life total, threat deck exhaustion). The catalogue below lists close to 290 mechanics with generic names, de-duplicated across the five research notes. Two findings matter most for Chief Complaint. First, the genre's central tension is **dilution versus thinning**, and its best-documented snowball brake is designer Donald X. Vaccarino's choice to make success clog the deck ([Secret History of Dominion](https://dominionstrategy.com/2013/06/24/the-secret-history-of-dominion/)). Second, **true hospital deck-builders barely exist in tabletop**: only Dynamite Nurse (2011) surfaced, so Chief Complaint's niche is largely open. The final section recommends six mechanics, in priority order, to add one at a time.

Space!), Imperium: Classics and Legends, and Orléans. **Co-operative, solo and campaign:** Aeon's End (including Legacy, Outcasts, New Age and Expedition mode), Spirit Island, Harry Potter: Hogwarts Battle, Legendary Encounters (Alien, Firefly), Arkham Horror LCG (as a deck-construction contrast), Shadowrun: Crossfire, Clank! Legacy, Slay the Spire: The Board Game, Hostage Negotiator, Paleo, the Lord of the Rings DBG and Hero Realms campaigns. **Variants:** Mystic Vale, The Quacks of Quedlinburg, Living Forest, Cubitos, Hardback, Xenon Profiteer, Dale of Merchants, Quarriors, Dice Masters, Dice Forge, Woodcraft, Altiplano, Fort, The Quest for El Dorado and Moonrakers. **Medical neighbours:** Dynamite Nurse, Pandemic: Hot Zone, Healthy Heart Hospital, Hospital Rush, Doctor Panic, Dice Hospital, Virus!, Critical Care and Hospital Life. The notes also name Great Western Trail, Star Wars: The Deckbuilding Game, Xenoshyft, Paperback, Earthborne Rangers and others only as unverified leads.

Three sources were unreachable, which limits depth on Dominion and on official definitions. The **Dominion Strategy wiki** sits behind an anti-bot check, so its content arrives through search snippets. **BoardGameGeek** mechanic and game pages returned HTTP 403, so BGG definitions are second-hand. **GDC Vault** needs a login. **Flag convention:** a mechanic marked **†** comes from the researchers' background knowledge and was *not* verified against a source; it is listed so the catalogue is complete, but treat it as a lead to check (for example in the Rio Grande rulebooks) before relying on it. Unmarked rows are sourced in the research notes; where a row shows no link, it shares the source given for the same game in a nearby row.

## The catalogue: fourteen families of mechanics

The family scheme follows the axes proposed in the taxonomy notes (acquisition, cycle, manipulation, play constraints, interaction, clock and victory), which mirror Engelstein and Shalev's split between Economics, Game End and Victory, and Card Mechanisms in *Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design* ([Deane Barker review](https://deanebarker.net/library/titles/building-blocks-game-design/)). BoardGameGeek files the whole genre under one umbrella, "Deck, Bag, and Pool Building", with Open Drafting and Hand Management as separate tags ([BGG blog post](https://boardgamegeek.com/blogpost/117626/mechanic-deck-bag-and-pool-building-3)); the Tallmove compendium places deck-building under "Engine Building" ([Tallmove](https://compendium.tallmove.com/mechanics/deck-building)). The scheme itself is the notes' own synthesis, not a quoted standard.

## Dilution sets the price of every purchase

The genre's single governing fact is that **every card dilutes every other card**: adding a card lowers how often all the rest appear, so indiscriminate buying weakens a deck, and every purchase is "a delayed investment" that pays only after the next reshuffle ([Tallmove](https://compendium.tallmove.com/mechanics/deck-building)). Thinning is the counterweight, which is why most deck-builders include a removal channel and why Slay the Spire turns removal into a paid service. Draw order is input randomness: players know the deck's composition and decide after seeing each hand, which keeps perceived agency high even though the draw is random (same source). The notes derive a corollary they could not source directly: because a card bought now first appears about one cycle (deck size divided by cards drawn per turn) later, late purchases are worth little, which explains the timing judgement around when to stop building.

**Big Money is the benchmark any design must beat.** The Dominion community treats "buy only treasure and Victory cards" as the floor for competent play: in a game without attacks it averages four Provinces by roughly turn 17, and Smithy-plus-Big-Money by roughly turn 14 ([DS wiki snippet](https://wiki.dominionstrategy.com/index.php/BMU); [Guide to the Base Game](https://dominionstrategy.com/2011/06/19/guide-to-the-dominion-base-game/)). Engines beat it only by combining villages, draw and payload, and they pay a set-up cost; an engine without payload loses to Big Money, and one that "greens" too early sputters ([Dominion 101](https://dominionstrategy.com/2017/11/06/dominion-101-what-is-an-engine/)). Trashers count as engine enablers because stop cards make drawing the deck fail. **Terminal collision** quantifies the action-budget pressure: a single terminal draw card of +1, +2 or +3 cards collides with another terminal roughly 34%, 38% and 44% of the time ([DS glossary](https://dominionstrategy.com/glossary/)), and a maths paper finds a second Smithy beats a Silver after turn 6 and a Gold after turn 8 ([Rose-Hulman UMJ](https://scholar.rose-hulman.edu/rhumj/vol25/iss1/2)). For an AP-limited game, those numbers argue that expensive single-use cards will often collide, and that AP-refunding cards are the lever. Chief Complaint's BalanceSim "never-buy bot" already fills the Big Money role as a tuning floor.

**Snowball brakes are designed in, not bolted on.** Vaccarino deliberately put Victory cards *into* the deck so that scoring makes the deck worse; he reworked the end condition from "any empty pile" to "Provinces or three piles" so that a Duchy rush alone couldn't end it; he settled the starter at 7 Copper and 3 Estate so starters would be junk; and he cut random self-trashing attacks because they could lock players out ([Secret History](https://dominionstrategy.com/2013/06/24/the-secret-history-of-dominion/)). The compendium adds that end triggers tied to supply depletion, which the leader can accelerate, and removal costs both damp runaway leaders, though a well-tuned early deck can still become "untouchable" ([Tallmove](https://compendium.tallmove.com/mechanics/deck-building)). The co-op family adds a third brake: **mirrored escalation**, where Aeon's End Expedition upgrades the nemesis deck in step with the treasures players earn ([Aeon's End Wiki](https://aeonsend.wiki.gg/wiki/Expedition)). The notes found no dedicated, citable article on catch-up mechanics or decision density, so those topics rest on the compendium and forum opinion.

**Market structure is a trade-off between planning and variety.** Fixed supply piles (Dominion) are easier to plan around; a rotating face-up row (Ascension, Star Realms) adds variety and denial but can lock a player out when everything is too expensive; and forum consensus favours a few always-available cards beside a dynamic row ([Tallmove](https://compendium.tallmove.com/mechanics/deck-building); [BGG thread, forum opinion](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/2480192/deckbuilders-static-or-dynamic-markets-which-do-yo)). Legendary's pre-chosen market is a third option. On the digital side, Slay the Spire's balancing tracked two metrics per card, how often it is picked when offered and how often it appears in winning decks, logged across every playtest ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/learn-i-slay-the-spire-i-s-metrics-driven-approach-to-game-balancing-at-gdc-2019)); the talk itself sits behind GDC Vault's login.

## Hospital deck-builders are almost absent from tabletop

The only clearly verified medical deck-builder is **Dynamite Nurse** (Arclight / Japanime Games, 2011), in which hospitals cure wounded heroes and pass troublesome patients to rivals; a blog adds that patient deaths give "death mark" cards and the player with the most receives a penalty card ([BGG](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgame/108341/dynamite-nurse/ratings?comment=1); [Geekly Grind](https://www.thegeeklygrind.com/all-posts/dynamite-nurse-board-game)). Its full rules were not retrieved. A list of 108 medical games identifies no other deck-builder ([Spotlight on Games](http://www.spotlightongames.com/list/medicine.html)). The medical neighbours use other mechanics: **Pandemic: Hot Zone** is action points, co-op and hand management ([Z-Man](https://www.zmangames.com/en/news/2020/5/7/hot-zone-series/)); **Healthy Heart Hospital** runs nine rounds of Ambulance, Player Action and Housekeeping phases with push-your-luck cube draws ([Armchair Dragoons](https://armchairdragoons.com/classic-reviews-healthy-heart-hospital/)); **Hospital Rush** is worker placement with "unfair" actions others can punish ([BGG](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgame/119265/hospital-rush)); **Doctor Panic** is a real-time co-op with an ER-manager role ([Board Game Rules](https://board-game-rules.com/boardgames/doctor-panic/)); **Dice Hospital** treats patients as dice in a worker-placement frame, with one unverified deck/bag/pool tag ([Alley Cat Games](https://www.alleycatgames.com/dice-hospital)); and **Virus!** uses a clean two-step state machine in which one virus infects an organ and a second destroys it, while one medicine protects and a second immunises ([UltraBoardGames](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/virus/game-rules.php)). Critical Care: The Game and Hospital Life are training-oriented and show no deck-building. Several other titles (Viral, Plague Inc: The Board Game, Med School, Nurse!) could not be verified. The practical conclusion: **Chief Complaint's niche, a medical deck-builder built around patient triage, is largely unoccupied**, and the borrowable ideas come from non-medical deck-builders plus Virus!'s state machine and Healthy Heart Hospital's arrivals phase.

## Fits for Chief Complaint

A check of the current code shows that Chief Complaint already uses more of this catalogue than its one-line description suggests. These are in place and are *not* recommendations:
- **Punishment junk with a removal channel (family 9).** A patient death adds a random Red Tape card (incident paperwork) to the discard pile (`GameManager.deathAddsRedTape`, `DeckManager.SpawnRandomRedTape`), and `TrashCardEffectSO` can dispose of Red Tape from hand. This is the Mage Knight / Arnak / Dynamite Nurse pattern.
- **Fixed supply with stock and staged unlocks (family 2).** The staff room sells from fixed piles with a maximum stock, restock and an escalation stage at which each pile unlocks (`SupplyRoom`), so the rotating-row lock-out problem doesn't arise.
- **Upgrade chain (family 6).** Cards upgrade into a named better version (`upgradesTo`), paid for with coloured upgrade tokens (`DeckUpgradeManager`), in the manner of Dominion's Travellers and Thunderstone's levelling.
- **Exhaust-on-use cards (family 6)**, an AP turn budget (family 4), patients with decaying stability (family 10 countdowns), escalation phases and Code Blues (family 10 staged threat), and Reputation as life (family 14).

The shelved earlier attempt added too many mechanics at once, so the list below is a queue, not a bundle. Add the first, rebalance it in `Tools/BalanceSim` against the never-buy bot, play it, then consider the next. The Papers, Please report's first recommendation, **Early Discharge**, can sit ahead of this queue: it is a board-side decision about patients, while everything below changes the player's turn, deck or staff room. Persistent unlocks, legacy content and between-game meta-progression from family 13 are deliberately excluded, because the game is not a roguelike.

| Priority | Mechanic (catalogue family) | Chief Complaint shape | Why it fits | Precedent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Prep-then-resolve investigations (4) | An "Order Blood Test" or "Order Imaging" card is played onto a patient this turn and reveals or diagnoses a symptom at the start of the next turn. | Models test turnaround and adds a planning decision against decaying stability, with no new resource or currency. It is one new `CardEffectSO` with a one-turn delay. | Aeon's End prepped spells; Spirit Island fast/slow |
| 2 | Telegraphed arrivals (10) | Show the next one or two incoming patients (and their arrival route: walk-in, ambulance or trauma flight) face up before they reach a department slot. | `PatientDeckManager.DrawRandomPatient` draws at the moment of arrival, and no preview was found. Telegraphing is the common tool that makes an automated opponent feel fair, and it turns escalation into something the player plans for. | Spirit Island invader track; Aeon's End power tokens; Healthy Heart Hospital's Ambulance phase |
| 3 | Events versus projects in the staff room (7) | Add Funding purchases that never enter the deck: one-shot "Events" (a single benefit next shift) and permanent "Projects" (a ward-wide rule bonus, such as +1 hand size in one department). | Gives Activity Funding an outlet that doesn't dilute the deck. Two cautions apply. **Any change touching Red Tape interacts with the BalanceSim's never-buy bot**: a bot that never buys may also never clear junk, so the target numbers (Funding 10, cap 15) need re-running after item 4. And **every junk card needs a visible removal channel**: every sourced junk design ships with one, and junk without an outlet reads as a lock-out, which is exactly the kind of attack Vaccarino cut. If Red Tape removal currently depends on drawing a specific trash card, check in play that it is reachable often enough.

## Conclusion

The catalogue shows that deck-building variety comes almost entirely from changing the edges of one loop (what enters the deck, what leaves it, what clogs it and what ends the game), not the loop itself. For Chief Complaint that is reassuring: the game already owns the core, and its most distinctive opportunity is thematic rather than structural, because no tabletop designer has yet put triage, test turnaround and clinical incidents into a deck. The strongest lesson from the sources is that the best-regarded designs pay for power with a deck cost (Victory cards that clog, Heat that the player chooses to spend, a Major power that makes you forget a card), so each Chief Complaint addition should carry its own price inside the deck, and should be measured against the never-buy benchmark before the next one is added.
